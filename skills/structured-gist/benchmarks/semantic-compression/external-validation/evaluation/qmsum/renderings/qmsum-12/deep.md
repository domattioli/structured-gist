- **Session 1: local-authority panel (Sharon Davies, Torfaen/ADEW; Nick Williams, Swansea/ADEW; David Hopkins, WLGA)**
  - **Drivers of rising exclusions**
    - i. Williams: more prevalent, very challenging behaviour across the system, often tied to ASD (autism spectrum disorder) and difficulty securing the right provision; Swansea's relevant numbers have risen roughly fourfold over five-six years; what looks like difficult behaviour is often additional learning needs, and system capacity hasn't caught up, pressuring both schools and EOTAS/PRU capacity
    - ii. Davies: Torfaen sees the same pattern and "seconds" Williams's account
  - **Reintegration strategies and continuum of provision**
    - i. Williams: Swansea has developed a new behaviour-and-well-being strategy with a tiered/staged approach including universal provision; similar strategies exist regionally (south-west and mid Wales) and, per the EOTAS network he chairs nationally, in many other local authorities; the direction has "moved forward" over the past two-three years but there's "a little bit of lag" bringing every authority to a consistent level
    - ii. some learners will always need more specialist provision beyond what mainstream staff/schools can be equipped to deliver
  - **Accountability measures**
    - i. Davies: schools face "a huge amount of pressures... to perform," and school culture plays into exclusion trends; interim measures (i.e., the shift toward the capped-9 attainment measure) are producing a cultural shift, but she expects "a lag in the system" before effects show up for learners; Torfaen has been "working really hard" on exclusions and ensuring a broad, balanced curriculum for every learner
    - ii. Williams (answering partly in Welsh): the messages government and local-authority directors/councils send matter — emphasizing children's future progression, not just reaching a fixed attainment level; Estyn itself has "changed its mind" and updated its messaging, and schools now better understand what's expected of them
  - **Parental engagement**
    - i. Williams: Swansea/Neath Port Talbot use a "team-around-the-family" approach, bringing multiple agencies together proactively, recognizing that behaviours seen at school usually run deeper in the home environment, requiring a whole-family support lens
    - ii. Davies and Williams agree the majority of Welsh local authorities now share this multi-agency understanding, working alongside social care and health colleagues on the "whole package" around families and learners
    - iii. Williams: extensive training across Wales on adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) and trauma-informed practice has changed how staff interpret behaviour — he recalls that as a teacher himself, he previously saw only "difficult behaviours" without understanding the background driving them; the profession is "more informed" now
  - **Funding (questions from Siân Gwenllian, with Sian Hughes)**
    - i. Davies: the incoming Additional Learning Needs (ALN) Act will add pressure to an already-stretched system; some Torfaen schools are behind on funding and forced into "hard choices" across every aspect of provision for learners
    - ii. Davies isn't sure ALN specifically is "worse affected" than other areas, but agrees more spending will likely be needed in light of the new Act; Williams agrees
    - iii. Williams: government funding announcements sometimes overlook PRU staff specifically, who may need even more training than mainstream school staff — government has since "addressed that" after being pushed, but it remains "a little bit of an afterthought," with more attention needed going forward
    - iv. Hopkins (WLGA), on delegation of funding to schools: delegation levels are "already very high" in most authorities, with agreements in place with government to devolve as much funding to schools as possible — he doesn't see delegation itself as a direct driver of exclusions; instead, he points to the narrow key-stage-4 end-point attainment measure as pressuring some headteachers toward exclusion or moving pupils to another school (which he calls "regrettable"), and separately flags that ALN funding is coming under increasing pressure — citing England's parallel legislative experience, where ALN-related tribunals became so burdensome that local authorities there "almost gave up going to tribunals" after repeatedly losing
    - v. Hopkins on whether new-Act pressures could mean more exclusions: "it shouldn't, but it could," depending on how headteachers/governing bodies react to financial and performance pressure; he hopes the new curriculum, alongside efforts with Estyn and Welsh Government to broaden accountability measures beyond narrow metrics, will let schools approach this more constructively; separately flags that ALN legislation extends support obligations up to age 25, adding pressure across the post-16 sector too
    - vi. Davies: variable, unpredictable provision needs (changing term to term, even week to week, as learners move in and out) make planning difficult and strain budgets that weren't allocated for unplanned arrivals
    - vii. Williams: PRUs have worked to ensure funding "follows" a child who moves out of mainstream school, since funding is only allocated annually, creating a lag risk when a school no longer educates a child but still holds that year's funding
  - **Collaborative working and transition arrangements (questions from Janet Finch-Saunders)**
    - i. Williams: PRU-sector networking has improved substantially — national PRU meetings, a regionally meeting EOTAS group, shared-practice conferences, joint provision visits, and closer curriculum-focused work between PRU and mainstream staff, aided by opportunities the new curriculum presents (contingent on funding and time); historically, staff sometimes lacked cross-setting experience in either direction
    - ii. on transition from PRU back to mainstream: Williams describes constituency-level problems where children can end up stuck at home between settings; Swansea has invested significant time and (via Band B capital funding) is building a brand-new PRU, opening January 2021; staff work closely with schools, using a part-time-timetable integration process with continued support after full return, gradually reduced — "proven very successful," though he's candid that reintegration becomes "far, far more difficult" for the most challenging, oldest (key-stage-4) learners, who are instead supported toward further education/college transitions
    - iii. Davies: Torfaen similarly leans on the team-around-the-family approach for reintegration, considering the "whole package" around a learner — including transport — to prepare everything needed for a successful return where possible; agrees key stage 4 is hardest
    - iv. Hopkins: at least one (unnamed — he says he's "not allowed to say") local authority is exploring retaining all pupils within mainstream school settings entirely; it's "early days," the principle is appealing but raises other funding/administrative issues; a similar model has been tried in England (Oxfordshire), though long-term outcomes there aren't well known to WLGA
    - v. Davies (drawing on her earlier career as an English primary headteacher): recalls Oxfordshire specifically considered dissolving its PRU and returning the money to secondary schools under a partnership arrangement with terms of reference (short of a formal service-level agreement), preventing schools from simply moving learners around; it "started off really well" some years ago, though she doesn't know if it continued
    - vi. Williams: Swansea has run a similar "devolve" (not "delegate," a distinction he stresses) model for almost three years, giving secondary schools extra resource plus a monitored action plan for how they use it — while conceding some learners will always need additional, bespoke support beyond what schools can provide; he calls Swansea's overall approach "a mixed economy"
  - **Delays accessing EOTAS provision**
    - i. Williams: capacity — insufficient spaces in some year groups — is the core cause; robust panels/systems try to ensure everything possible is attempted within the home school first, sometimes via a (non-ideal, but family-and-youngster-informed) part-time timetable; Swansea has "reinvigorated" its managed-move program (with a dedicated overseer working closely with secondary schools) to give learners a genuine "second chance," including a cooling-off period if a learner prefers their original school — learner voice is treated as important, requiring "quite wide" provision options
  - **Commissioning frameworks and approved-provider status**
    - Williams: local authorities already run such frameworks, constrained by a shortage of additional providers and the priority placed on safeguarding — a "balancing act" between rigor and not discouraging providers from coming forward; beyond safeguarding, training/support for staff in that additional provision remains "a struggle" given existing capacity pressures on in-house provision and oversight visits
  - **Local-authority awareness and quality assurance of school-organized EOTAS**
    - i. Williams (Swansea only): schools must report EOTAS-provision locations as part of a required plan, and challenge advisers monitor/quality-assure this during school visits
    - ii. on safeguarding of privately run/independent EOTAS: Williams affirms schools take safeguarding "very, very seriously" as a "prime driver," though he can't speak beyond Swansea
    - iii. on local-authority quality-assurance role for school-organized EOTAS: Williams notes much additional provision (e.g., a mechanics course via a college) is also used directly by the local authority itself, so it gets "quality assured... by two sides"
    - iv. Davies: Torfaen's secondary schools run their own Torfaen Curriculum Panel (TCP) reviewing alternative provision, attended by senior leaders, currently undergoing a review of whether provision that worked in the past still fits current, more complex learner needs, whether providers still offer what's sought, whether the curriculum offer and staff training are right, and generally "keeping that momentum going" rather than assuming past success guarantees present fitness
  - **Individual/home tuition quality assurance (clarified as meaning home tuition specifically, per the chair)**
    - i. Davies: local authorities are legally "restricted" in how much access they get into a family's home, making quality assurance difficult unless families themselves invite them in
    - ii. Williams: Swansea uses only local-authority-employed home-tuition staff (never agency staff) for children who, for reasons like school anxiety or medical need, currently can't access school or approved provision — he doesn't know if this is standard practice across Wales, only for Swansea; Davies confirms Torfaen is "very similar"
    - iii. Hopkins: historically, directly provided/commissioned home tuition would be quality-assured as a matter of course; the harder question is other, third-party-commissioned forms of provision, where commissioning arrangements should require quality safeguards but monitoring is the real challenge — he can't give a definitive Wales-wide answer
  - **WLGA paper concern on EOTAS pupil registration changes**
    - Williams (on a joint ADEW/WLGA paper flagging concern about "potential impact of changes to the registration of pupils who are EOTAS" for data/funding/joint working): stresses the importance of funding following the learner and of schools retaining "shared ownership," since EOTAS placement is meant to be temporary and reintegration-focused for most learners; connects this to the committee's earlier questions about "off-rolling" at GCSE stage to keep pupils out of school performance data, which local-authority messaging (to governors, headteachers, staff) actively works against — framing EOTAS learners as "our learners," the "most vulnerable" the system has, with direct links (if unsupported) to crime and broader societal cost, making it "in all our interests" to do right by them
  - **Curriculum access and consistency (questions from Siân Gwenllian)**
    - i. Hopkins: distinguishes PRUs (which follow a set, inspected curriculum under local-authority control — WLGA is "happy and content" there) from local-authority-commissioned home tuition (which should have safeguards, though what actually happens in the home can't always be guaranteed) from third-party-commissioned provision (hardest to vouch for — commissioning terms should require quality safeguards, but monitoring is the open question); calls this "very general, broad-brush" and defers specifics to his co-panelists
    - ii. asked directly whether this variability worries the WLGA, Hopkins confirms: "of course it's a concern... if you can't guarantee quality in any shape or form for a young person"
    - iii. Williams: regional collaboration on staff training (including joint work with school staff) is "quite positive," though "there is work to do"; PRUs currently deliver broad-and-balanced curriculum by requirement, including literacy, numeracy, and Welsh — learners aren't "out all the time"
    - iv. Davies: reiterates collaboration between PRUs and schools as the key mechanism for ensuring learners get what they need
    - v. Gwenllian presses that this describes best practice under direct local-authority control, but asks about inconsistency beyond PRUs (home/private provision) — whether the panel is presenting only the "best elements" while under-the-surface problems go unexamined; Williams responds that "consistency is developing across Wales" in curriculum and collaboration terms — not perfect, but improving

  - **Support and capacity (questions from Dawn Bowden)**
    - i. Bowden raises Hopkins's earlier ALN point directly: is ALN support especially hard to secure in EOTAS settings specifically, since EOTAS learners often have particular needs that make EOTAS placement necessary in the first place?
    - ii. Hopkins: within tightly regulated settings (PRUs, local-authority-commissioned/delivered tuition), needs "definitely should be being met," though he can't confirm they all are; outside that tightly regulated part of the system, meeting individual needs becomes "increasingly difficult" — he flags this as a likely genuine issue, deferring to colleagues for more detail
    - iii. Bowden presses further: there's a known "disconnect" between what should happen and what does — even within local-authority PRUs, the full curriculum (beyond basic numeracy/literacy/well-being) isn't always delivered, and provision quality varies even in commissioned EOTAS settings
    - iv. Hopkins: variation exists, but within the local-authority-maintained sector, provision "should be being made," and if not, the local authority should be aware and address it
    - v. Williams: references Swansea's own inspection history — provision that fell short "some years ago" now inspects "very positive," though he stresses "we're not being complacent"; the biggest ongoing challenge is capacity, tied to funding — he'd like a dedicated educational psychologist working solely with PRU/EOTAS learners, but current system-wide demand doesn't allow it
    - vi. Davies: agrees capacity is central but stresses it's also about retaining the *right* staff — specialist expertise (built over years) is "quite niche," and when an expert person moves on or retires, rebuilding that capability "from scratch" is genuinely difficult; it's "more than just one person," requiring a whole surrounding training package
    - vii. Williams: proposes building EOTAS/PRU exposure into initial teacher training (currently, newly qualified teachers can't work directly in this provision — arguably reasonable, since they should establish mainstream experience first, but a future specialist pipeline is still needed) and into ongoing professional development, with two-way exchange (PRU staff visiting schools and vice versa) to counter the professional isolation that can come with long PRU tenures; flags this as a discussion topic for the EOTAS network he chairs
    - viii. Bowden references prior Estyn evidence about small PRU staff numbers limiting access to professional training, and asks if that matches the panel's experience and what could be done; Davies: the picture is "limited" but "getting better," citing growing networks and conferences
    - ix. Bowden asks whether local authorities can encourage specialist staff (teachers, educational psychologists) to share expertise with independent EOTAS providers; Williams: again a capacity conundrum — pulling specialists out of provision is hard to backfill (e.g., via supply teachers) given existing demands
    - x. Davies: suggests creating an explicit specialization pathway/offer for staff who want to move further into this area, which currently doesn't formally exist
  - **Mental health access (questions from Dawn Bowden)**
    - i. Williams: mental-health-service access varies even within his own region, which spans three separate health boards; his own region's access "hasn't been great, to be honest," though a positive step is the new requirement for each health board to have a "DECLO" (designated education clinical lead officer — Williams can't recall the exact term on the spot), with whom his authority has already met; he expects stronger future partnerships, alongside the local authority's own universal well-being strategy (including counselling) designed to reach learners appropriately at different stages without over-referring
    - ii. Bowden asks whether a lack of mainstream mental-health support could itself be pushing more learners toward EOTAS; Hopkins: suspects a "general deficit" in Child and Adolescent Mental Health Services (CAMHS) capacity broadly (not a criticism of the service itself, just its scale), which likely translates into pressure on learners with additional needs regardless of setting — though he has no direct evidence base to offer
  - **Teacher training and workforce sustainability (Siân Gwenllian supplementary)**
    - i. Gwenllian asks whether new teachers, even without direct EOTAS training, are otherwise equipped to handle rising challenging behaviour generally, given how many leave the profession over exactly this; suggests the system needs a more holistic response
    - ii. Williams: attributes attrition to more than challenging behaviour alone — notes increased school placement time for trainees now helps, alongside needing both theory and mentored practice, plus consistent school-wide behaviour policy
    - iii. Gwenllian: presses that losing experienced staff while fewer enter the profession compounds the "bigger picture" challenge; Davies agrees ("Yes, certainly")
  - **Gwent-specific follow-ups (chair, Lynne Neagle)**
    - i. chair notes her understanding that the CAMHS in-reach project operating in Gwent doesn't include PRUs, and asks Davies to confirm; Davies isn't sure
    - ii. chair separately asks about the Gwent attachment team's work with PRUs and its impact on staff support/embedding trauma awareness; Davies: any professional learning is positive, calling it "positive impact" though "early days" and the "overall impact" isn't yet known
  - **Rural transport (Janet Finch-Saunders)**
    - i. Williams: cites Powys as an example — provision spans from near Oswestry in the north to Ystradgynlais in the south, with separate north/south PRU provision but still enormous travel distances for learners; contrasts this with more geographically compact Torfaen, where he'd guess (deferring to Davies) transport is less of an issue
    - ii. Hopkins: not personally aware of transport issues but hasn't specifically investigated, and is open to looking into it

- **Session 2: Samaritans / Mind Cymru panel (Sarah Stone, Samaritans Cymru; Liz Williams, Samaritans; Dr Ian Johnson, Mind Cymru) — sixth evidence session of the inquiry**
  - **Drivers of rising exclusions**
    - i. Johnson: calls this "a very difficult subject" given limited visibility into underlying reasons; statistical releases give only reductionist categories (persistent disruptive behaviour, verbal abuse, physical assaults) without the deeper causal story, even though (drawing on his own experience as a recent primary/secondary school governor) schools generate a substantial paper trail before any exclusion — that detail "doesn't seem to really find its way up to a higher level"
    - ii. on rising exclusions among younger children specifically: Johnson finds this "very concerning" and notes a substantial recent increase, but the supporting evidence remains largely anecdotal (behavioural/underlying issues, growing ACEs awareness); he can't yet explain why primary schools specifically appear to be excluding more
    - iii. Stone: Samaritans' original report on school exclusions began from anecdotal reports of individual distress reaching the charity via projects working with excluded young people, including primary-school-age children; the report's central message is that this raises "serious questions" the sector "doesn't know the answer" to without dedicated further work, given the impact on affected young people's life trajectories
  - **Accountability measures and off-rolling**
    - i. Johnson: cites Estyn's pupil-registration-practices report (October of the prior year) on off-rolling — a "strong impact" specifically between year 10 and 11 (~4% of pupils off-rolled), alongside a steadier baseline of 1.5-2% off-rolled in other transition years (year 8/9, year 9/10); based on cohorts of roughly 30,000-32,000, that steadier rate implies 500-600 children off-rolled in any given year outside the year-10/11 spike — a pattern he calls concerning specifically because of its "normalisation" throughout the secondary system; calls the existing Estyn work "quite factual," focused on numbers, and argues qualitative work is needed to understand the story behind it and whether it connects to accountability-measure incentives, including anticipated effects of the shift to capped-9
    - ii. Liz Williams: raises that schools are measured "only... on academic outcomes rather than the journey travelled by the child and the efforts put in... to nurture... well-being," particularly for vulnerable pupils — meaning there's currently limited incentive to retain challenging or lower-attaining pupils, something she says needs further scrutiny
    - iii. Stone: cross-refers the committee to the Welsh Government's loneliness and isolation strategy, to which Samaritans made extensive representations; the strategy already contains a commitment to "use... approach to accountability to recognise inclusive schools and reduce incentives to remove pupils from schools" — Stone calls this "a really important commitment to make a reality," given what she frames as currently "perverse" incentives around the issue
    - iv. Johnson adds a further open question: what happens differently to children who enter PRU/EOTAS provision earlier in their school career (years 7/8, 8/9) and loop back into mainstream, versus those who enter nearer the end of compulsory schooling and what becomes of them afterward — flagged as something the committee may pick up later in the session
  - **Support for pupils at risk of becoming EOTAS (Janet Finch-Saunders)**
    - i. Stone: this was a "major focus" of Samaritans' work, which concluded support currently "isn't" adequate, and that the issue is "not simply a question of the young person" but of the whole school environment's interaction with a young person's challenges; calls for a compassionate, confident, whole-school response to distress (which may not present as obvious distress, and connects to responding to disclosed suicidal thoughts); stresses that school may be a young person's "only safe place," a point many headteachers/teachers already grasp — citing inspiring restorative-justice approaches used by some Cardiff schools as a promising model, and noting strong educator interest in the report's findings since publication; frames the real opportunity as building on and generalizing existing good practice, given the severe, hard-to-reverse lifetime consequences (including elevated suicide risk) of not holding young people within education
    - ii. Johnson: stresses the need to address both the individual and the "macro" picture — a statutory whole-school approach, echoing the committee's prior 'Mind over matter' work, so prevention operates across the entire school community from the earliest years through secondary school, keeping learner support "at the forefront" of how schools interpret pupil behaviour, tying back into ACEs awareness
  - **Nine Samaritans recommendations (Sarah Stone)**
    - recognize ACE impact; train teachers toward a confident, understanding response; learn from what already works; listen to young people's own voices (building on the committee's own prior direct-voice work); help young people build their own emotional literacy/self-awareness (citing international evidence on its value, developed alongside Wales's new curriculum health work); make health-and-well-being/mental-health content statutory within the new curriculum specifically to avoid entrenching inequality between schools with the "good will" to act and those that find it "harder" — summarized as "a menu," grounded in respecting and working with teachers' and schools' existing efforts and intentions
    - Johnson adds: identifying "the best support at this point in time" must be individualized, learner-voice-driven; flags an upcoming Mind Cymru "inclusive education inquiry" project specifically foregrounding young people's own voices in the evidence base, since current evidence is mostly data-driven or anecdotal; notes ongoing work still needed connecting PRU/EOTAS provision to the new curriculum
  - **Diagnosis vs. support funding balance (Janet Finch-Saunders)**
    - Liz Williams: raised in Samaritans' research-stage round table — participants were concerned about limited awareness/knowledge around additional learning needs, since these learners are both at elevated exclusion risk and over-represented among those excluded, especially "cusp" or undiagnosed cases (citing ADD/ADHD as common in the excluded cohort); diagnosis alone has limited value without proper understanding and support behind it — one participant estimated up to 50% of a classroom could have an additional learning need, meaning diagnosis without adequate support doesn't actually benefit the child; the deeper takeaway participants emphasized was the importance of genuinely inclusive schools giving every learner proper opportunity to progress, diagnosed or not
  - **Awareness of early trauma / ACEs (Janet Finch-Saunders)**
    - i. Johnson: can't speak to individual-school awareness specifically, but notes all-Wales work on the ACEs agenda is underway (citing his own former school, now trauma-informed and widening that work); flags a specific concern about EOTAS/PRU progress within the incoming curriculum, referencing education-minister comments (in response to Suzy Davies, January statement) about "disallowing" curriculum areas for individual learners — which he has no objection to in principle, but stresses the health-and-well-being learning area and mental-health/emotional-well-being content must stay "centre stage" for this especially vulnerable group, a matter he expects the committee to revisit during the forthcoming curriculum and assessment Bill
    - ii. Stone: connects early trauma to loneliness/isolation's role in driving distress and suicidal ideation, citing research on "belongingness" as critical to human functioning; notes Samaritans is releasing forthcoming research specifically on middle-aged men whose early-years trajectories trace back to this dynamic, and connects higher exclusion rates among boys to disproportionately high suicide rates and related issues among men — urging the committee to see "this whole picture" and to recognize young people's own strength/resilience as an asset worth building on, framing this as a genuine opportunity for positive change (Finch-Saunders notes north Wales suicide figures specifically are "very worrying")
    - iii. Liz Williams: previews the same forthcoming men's research as showing how often these men "fell through the net" repeatedly as children, with many missed intervention opportunities in cycles of inequality — reinforcing that early challenging behaviour is "the first signs of distress," making this fundamentally a preventative-agenda issue
  - **Current mental-health support levels (Dawn Bowden)**
    - i. Johnson: can't fully answer what's currently happening, citing the absence of a national data/information picture — part of why the push toward embedding this in the curriculum has grown
    - ii. Bowden notes much of the panel's evidence is anecdotal (referrals, own research); Johnson confirms the planned Mind Cymru research targets exactly this evidence gap; notes rising self-harm concern generally, with a gendered pattern (an increasing focus on self-harm among girls aged 15-19 specifically); from his prior committee appearance (Time to Change Wales project) his experience is that outcomes depend heavily on a school's senior-management-team commitment — strong SMT leadership produces real progress, more "laissez-faire" SMTs produce less — reinforcing his case for statutory whole-school-approach guidance to avoid uneven outcomes
    - iii. Bowden asks specifically whether progress in mainstream schools is following through into PRU/EOTAS settings; Johnson is candid he "couldn't give... an answer" right now, calling the inability to get a national feel for this "slightly concerning," while suspecting pockets of good regional/national practice exist that deserve wider visibility
  - **Off-rolling of pupils with mental-health problems (Dawn Bowden)**
    - i. Liz Williams: confirms this "certainly does happen," drawing on both Samaritans research and her own prior work in pupil referral units with vulnerable children; describes a pattern where children with undiagnosed learning/communication difficulties get labelled "naughty and disruptive," become anxious and unable to keep up, act out specifically to escape the classroom, and are consequently off-rolled rather than supported; cites a positive counter-example — a PRU where children became distressed and disruptive specifically on Friday afternoons, which staff correctly read (rather than dismissing as "ridiculous" disruption) as anticipatory distress about unsafe/unfed weekends at home, responding with compassion instead of discipline; stresses teachers "shouldn't have to do this alone" and need proper training/confidence, including to ask direct questions about self-harm or suicidal intent when warning signs appear (referencing Samaritans' "compassion in education" toolkit); concludes that without properly equipped/trained staff, off-rolling — especially at key stage 4, where it protects a school's attainment data — carries little disincentive as things stand
    - ii. Bowden asks how prevalent the "naughty" labelling pattern is; Liz Williams: "absolutely" prevalent, driven by individual school/teacher experience — children (especially those with undiagnosed communication difficulties who otherwise seem "bright") get labelled naughty rather than understood as anxious or affected by unseen ACEs at home; notes children can also learn to "play up to" that label if acting out reliably gets them out of an anxiety-inducing classroom
    - iii. Bowden observes that the "naughty" label itself becomes the barrier to getting proper support; Liz Williams agrees, noting formal diagnosis "opens... doors" to school support, and reiterates that embedding statutory emotional/mental-health content into school culture helps students themselves recognize when and how to seek help
    - iv. Johnson: shares a Mind Cymru focus-group finding — a boy reluctant to disclose problems to a teacher who held both his pastoral and disciplinary roles, for fear of disbelief or being seen as making excuses for "poor behaviour" — a consideration he says shouldn't be over-generalized but matters from a learner's perspective; returns briefly to the off-rolling data (repeating the Estyn year-10/11 and steadier 1.5-2% figures, and the unexplained 500-600-per-year baseline), stressing the qualitative "why" remains unanswered, including how much relates to mental-health factors versus accountability-driven behavior at year 10/11 specifically
  - **Welsh-medium provision (Dawn Bowden)**
    - Johnson: hasn't specifically looked for evidence here, but notes Estyn's report found generally fewer off-rollings from Welsh-medium education into EOTAS — possibly reflecting a complete lack of local EOTAS provision in some Welsh-medium areas, or a different socio-economic profile (since exclusion correlates strongly with free-school-meal eligibility, and Welsh-medium schools' catchments can differ socio-economically); no specific work appears to have examined Welsh-medium EOTAS needs/provision; his unevidenced suspicion is that it's handled inconsistently, locally, likely more prevalent (by incidental local capacity) in west and north-west Wales
  - **Delays entering EOTAS provision (Dawn Bowden)**
    - Stone: no specific research on this exact question, but frames delay through a broader lens: strong links exist between inequality and exclusion, with the most excludable young people also often carrying other disadvantages; any period out of education/support exacerbates pre-existing inequality and increases vulnerability through separation from a peer group, pushing young people to seek "alternative community"; cites a surge of interest since the report's publication from bodies worried about crime — Samaritans has engaged the youth justice board and police over "county lines" and home-growing drug operations exploiting exactly these disengaged young people; cites a recent Public Health Wales/Swansea University child-death review covering 33 youth suicide/suspected-suicide deaths, whose findings/recommendations noted these young people were "not... held" in education, training, or employment and had "slipped through" multiple systems, making the core message about not letting these gaps lengthen especially urgent
  - **Individual/home tuition well-being impact (Dawn Bowden)**
    - i. Liz Williams: no direct evidence on well-being impact specifically, but flags as a significant, related concern that there's no central data on how many young people are home-schooled — making this population potentially "hidden or invisible" and at heightened risk of exclusion-related harms; also flags uncertainty about how (or whether) unregistered children re-enter schooling if that's judged best for them; reiterates the "men's research" finding that home schooling might serve academic needs while leaving peer-group isolation and loneliness unaddressed, mirroring the later-life difficulty seen in men who lacked same-age peer interaction growing up
    - ii. Johnson: distinguishes two related questions (delay before entering provision, and long-term effects of individual tuition) — individual tuition can be genuinely beneficial where a learner can't function in a group educational setting, but may be inappropriate longer-term, and is "very much an individual matter"; his main concern is the gap itself between leaving mainstream education and entering any EOTAS provision (due to capacity or other reasons) — while causation/exacerbation of mental-health issues within EOTAS isn't established, it "seems intuitive" that such a gap is unhelpful, both for education-rights reasons and for the emotional state of young people likely already in a "confused and troubled" place during that gap; references a 2016 Estyn report on EOTAS recommending CAMHS support within such settings, without knowing what progress (if any) has followed, and flags an open question about the overlap between mental-health and neurodevelopmental needs in this population
    - iii. Stone: notes the loneliness-and-isolation strategy already commits to analyzing existing education and health data to explore correlations between EOTAS/exclusion and mental well-being (including loneliness/isolation specifically) — a "welcome" commitment, while stressing there may be both positive and negative effects still unknown, given how much remains unexamined overall
  - **Post-exclusion support and responsibility (Siân Gwenllian)**
    - i. Gwenllian asks generally whether excluded young people get adequate support, not just educationally, and whether responsibility for coordinating it is clear
    - ii. Johnson: suspects support happens locally in places but there's no national picture; notes the gap between exclusion and entering EOTAS provision (echoing earlier capacity-issue discussion) as itself evidence of what happens to these especially vulnerable children while "outside of the system"; on responsibility, states it formally rests with the local education authority, but stresses these children typically touch multiple institutions, so services must be "interwoven" around them; connects this to his planned "inclusive education" inquiry work, flags funding adequacy as a live concern, and suggests thinking of the system similarly to "corporate parenting" (with local-authority figures available on this, though he can't recall them offhand)
    - iii. Liz Williams: adds that responsibility also partly sits with parents — but parents can only support a child (whether at exclusion risk or already in EOTAS) effectively if they themselves have adequate information about their child's educational rights and entitlements, which she notes likely varies by school/local authority
  - **Closing summary contributions**
    - i. Johnson: stresses ensuring the new curriculum's EOTAS provision genuinely includes the whole-school approach, and that Welsh-medium availability is addressed nationally (possibly via local consortia), alongside how initial and ongoing teacher training (for both new and experienced teachers) will actually implement this — given that EOTAS learners tend to be especially vulnerable, prioritizing well-being alongside qualifications/certificates matters as a "vital point for the years to come"
    - ii. asked specifically about professional-development recommendations, Liz Williams: reiterates the need to embed the inequality/challenging-behaviour link into training so it lessens exclusion risk, and specifically that mental-health training should be built into *initial* teacher training rather than remaining "supplementary," giving teachers both skill and confidence
    - iii. Johnson: reiterates that statutory status for the whole-school approach ensures consistent knowledge/awareness/signposting throughout a school community, embedding mental-health/emotional-well-being "first," rather than "waiting for a problem," as "the key to improving the well-being of our future generations"
    - iv. Stone: closes by hoping the wider community recognizes the inequality/ACEs link and the opportunity this represents to intervene before a young person falls "out of school, and out of anything, and out of sight"
